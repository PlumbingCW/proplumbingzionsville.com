Tankless Water Heater for Shower: Sizing in Zionsville, IN.

A tankless water heater can run a shower for as long as you like, because it heats water as it flows rather than draining a stored supply. The catch is flow rate. If the heater cannot heat enough gallons per minute, the shower runs lukewarm, and that shows up fastest in a Zionsville, IN winter.

This guide is about sizing for showers. We cover the flow of common showerheads, how to add up what runs at the same time, and how the temperature of the incoming water changes what a unit can deliver. Our tankless water heater page covers installation.

How much water does a shower actually use?

The EPA says standard showerheads use 2.5 gallons per minute (gpm). WaterSense labeled showerheads must stay at or below 2.0 gpm. Showering accounts for nearly 17 percent of indoor residential water use, about 40 gallons per day for an average family.

A tankless heater is sized in gpm, so these two numbers are the starting point. One 2.5 gpm shower asks for 2.5 gpm of hot-mixed water. Switch to a 2.0 gpm head and the demand drops by a fifth.

Add up what runs at the same time.

Size the heater for the worst moment, not the average one. This table shows the arithmetic with common flows.

Scenario.Fixtures running.Total flow needed.
One WaterSense shower.One 2.0 gpm head.2.0 gpm.
One standard shower.One 2.5 gpm head.2.5 gpm.
Two showers at once.Two 2.0 gpm heads.4.0 gpm.
Two standard showers.Two 2.5 gpm heads.5.0 gpm.
Shower plus a sink.2.5 gpm head and a 1.5 gpm bathroom faucet.4.0 gpm.

Those totals are flow after mixing hot and cold, so the heater delivers somewhat less hot water than the total. Your plumber will convert that using your target temperature.

Temperature rise is the hidden variable.

A tankless unit's gpm rating depends on how much it must heat the water. Temperature rise is the target temperature minus the incoming water temperature. For example, water arriving at 40 F and heated to 105 F is a 65 degree rise. This is an illustration, not a Zionsville measurement.

A bigger rise means a lower flow from the same unit. The box often lists a high flow at a modest rise. Compare units at your rise, not at the best-case number printed in large type.

How to size a tankless for your showers.

You can do the first four steps before anyone visits. The last one is for the plumber.

1

List the showerheads.

Check the flow stamped on each head or the box. Multi-head setups and rain heads add up fast.

2

Decide what runs together.

Two showers at once, a shower with the dishwasher, or laundry during a morning rush. Pick the heaviest realistic case.

3

Add the flows.

Use the table above and write down the peak gpm.

4

Think winter.

Incoming water is coldest in winter, so a unit that works in July may struggle in January. Size for the cold season.

5

Let the plumber check the gas and vent.

A unit with a large flow rating also needs a gas line and vent built for it.

Shower problems tankless can cause, and fixes.

Most complaints come from sizing or fixture mismatches, not from the heater itself.

  • Shower runs lukewarm The heater is undersized for the rise, or two fixtures are drawing at once. Lower the flow or stagger use.
    .
  • Hot, cold, hot The unit shuts off at very low flow and re-lights. A very low-flow showerhead or a half-open valve can cause it.
    .
  • Wait before hot arrives Cold water in the pipe between heater and shower must clear first. A recirculation loop shortens it.
    .
  • Scale buildup In hard water, mineral deposits coat the heat exchanger. A scheduled descaling flush keeps the output up.
    .

Tankless or a tank for shower-heavy households.

Lifespan favors tankless, which DOE puts at about 20 years against 10 to 15 for a tank. Daily habits decide the rest.

Tankless suits.

  • Long showers back to back.
  • One or two bathrooms in use at a time.
  • Small closets where a tank crowds the space.
  • Owners planning a long stay.

A tank suits.

  • Several showers starting at once.
  • Low gas capacity at the meter.
  • Homes where a vent route is difficult.
  • Short-term ownership.

Scalding and safety checks.

A tankless unit can deliver water hot enough to scald in seconds if its temperature is set high. Check the setting on the display, and keep it lower around children and older adults. Run the shower for a minute and test with your hand before stepping in.

Gas units depend on correct venting. A gas smell means everyone leaves, and the call to 911 or the gas utility happens outside. Battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms, per the CPSC, belong on each floor and near bedrooms.

Call a plumber or DIY.

You can swap a showerhead, read the flow stamp and run the arithmetic yourself. Choosing the unit, sizing the gas line, venting it and connecting the water all belong to a licensed plumber.

Before hiring, look the plumber up in the state's license verification tool. If you already own a tankless and the shower has gone cold, our water heater repair page covers common faults like scale and a failed flame sensor.

It does show the season when your heater works hardest, so size for January, not for a July shower.

Permits in town run through the Planning & Building Department on Oak Street. A tankless install that changes gas piping or venting may call for one, and that is a question for the Town, not a guess from us.

Hardness matters to a shower unit because scale coats the heat exchanger. Your water bill names your utility, and the utility's annual water quality report lists hardness. We have not confirmed the hardness of Zionsville water, so rely on that report, not a neighbor's guess.

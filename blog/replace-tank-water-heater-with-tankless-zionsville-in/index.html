Replace Tank Water Heater With Tankless in Zionsville, IN.

Replacing a tank water heater with a tankless one means more than lifting out a cylinder and hanging a box on the wall. A tankless unit heats water as it flows, so the gas line, vent, power and water connections around it often change. For a Zionsville, IN home, the real question is whether the upgrade fits how you use hot water and how long you plan to stay.

Below we cover how to make that decision, the upgrades that can come with a conversion, and the lifespan numbers from the US Department of Energy. Our tankless water heater page explains the installation itself.

Keep a tank or convert? A side-by-side look.

Neither choice is right for every house. This table lays out the trade-offs that matter in practice.

Question.Stay with a tank.Convert to tankless.
How long does it last?DOE lists storage tanks at 10 to 15 years.DOE lists tankless at about 20 years.
Where does hot water come from?A stored supply, say 40 or 50 gallons, that can run out.Heated on demand, so it does not run out, but flow is limited.
What changes in the house?Usually the same hookups as the old tank.Gas line size, vent type, power and drain often change.
Where does it sit?On the floor, needs a pan or drain nearby.Wall mounted, frees floor space.
What does maintenance look like?Anode rod check, flushing sediment.Periodic descaling flush of the heat exchanger.
What fails the plan?Hot water runs out when many people shower.Too many fixtures running at once for the unit's flow.

Why lifespan matters more than the sticker.

A tankless unit costs more up front and needs more installation work. What it can give back is time. DOE lists tankless water heaters at about 20 years and storage tanks at 10 to 15, so over two decades you might buy one tankless or buy a tank twice.

That math only works if you stay put and the unit stays healthy. Heat pump water heaters sit at 10 to 15 years in the same DOE list, so the longest life belongs to tankless.

Upgrades a tank-to-tankless conversion can require.

Ask your plumber to walk through each of these before you accept a quote. Any one of them can change the scope of the job.

1

A bigger gas line.

A tankless burner draws far more gas at once than a tank burner. The existing line may be too small and need to be upsized from the meter.

2

New venting.

Many tankless units are sealed combustion and vent through stainless steel or plastic pipe to an outside wall or roof. The old tank flue is often not usable.

3

Power.

A gas tankless still needs an electrical outlet for its fan and controls. If none is nearby, one has to be added.

4

Condensate drain.

A condensing model makes acidic water that must drain to a floor drain or a neutralizer.

5

Valves and relief.

Isolation valves with service ports let you descale the unit, and the relief valve still needs a safe discharge pipe.

6

Old tank removal.

The tank, the old flue and its gas connection are taken out, and the wall or floor is patched.

Who converts happily and who should skip it.

A house with a few people and spread-out showers can feel different from one with heavy hot water use at the same time.

Tankless tends to fit when.

  • You plan to stay in the home for many years.
  • The gas meter and line can handle the load.
  • Hot water runs one or two fixtures at a time.
  • You want floor space back in a closet or basement.

A tank may be the better call when.

  • You may sell within a few years.
  • Gas line and vent would need major rebuilding.
  • Several showers and the dishwasher often overlap.
  • Water is very hard and you will not maintain the unit.

What tankless does not fix.

Tankless does not make hot water arrive sooner at a far bathroom. The pipe between the heater and the faucet still holds cold water that must run out first. A recirculation loop or a point-of-use heater helps with that, not the main unit alone.

It also needs a minimum flow to fire. A trickle from a half-open tap may leave you with lukewarm water. And when two showers and a washing machine call at once, the unit may split the heat thin.

Gas safety during a conversion.

Never cut or cap a gas line yourself. A gas smell at any point means getting out first, then calling the gas utility or 911 from outside, without stopping to flip switches.

The Consumer Product Safety Commission advises CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas, battery-operated or with battery backup, preferably interconnected. Separate from any plumbing work, it is worth checking them now.

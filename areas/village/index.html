Plumber in the Village, Zionsville, IN.

The Village is the name Zionsville residents use for the historic downtown, the Main Street business blocks and the older streets of houses around them. This page is for homeowners in that part of town.

Older neighborhoods bring a different set of plumbing questions than a new subdivision does. We separate what public records say about the Village from general plumbing information, and we list what we could not confirm.

What the Village means in Zionsville.

The Town's zoning code uses the word directly. It has a Village Residential district, whose stated purpose is to maintain the historic core or village neighborhoods, and an Urban Village Business district for the downtown business center.

The Village is not an official boundary on its own, because zoning districts are defined by map. Local press uses the term for the five-block business district. In 2025 Main Street Zionsville received the state's Indiana Main Street designation.

The historic district and its status.

A Zionsville Historic District nomination to the National Register of Historic Places describes an area roughly bounded by Ash and Sycamore Streets on the north and south, and Elm and 6th Streets on the east and west. The nomination is pending. It was on the Indiana review board agenda for October 14, 2026, and we have not confirmed the outcome, so please do not read it as a listing.

We also did not find an adopted local historic district. A conservation district for Village homes has been discussed, but we cannot say it is in force. We found no rules limiting ordinary plumbing repairs, though for work that changes the outside of a house, ask the Town building department first.

The homes.

The nomination counts about 500 primary buildings in the district, around 400 of them homes, with bungalows making up about a quarter of the dwellings. Examples it describes range from houses built in the 1860s to a 1962 brick ranch.

The nomination says streets and most alleys are paved, and many are narrow and tree lined with sidewalks. It does not give lot sizes or describe the plumbing in the houses, so we will not guess at pipe materials.

What older homes often call about.

These are general patterns for older houses, not a count of calls in the Village.

1

Drains that clog again and again.

Older drain lines can be rougher inside, which catches debris. A drain cleaning visit clears it, and a camera can show the cause.

2

Mature trees near the sewer line.

Roots can enter a joint in any line. A sewer inspection shows what the line looks like before you decide on a repair.

3

Old supply pipe and low water pressure.

Pipe material varies from house to house, and age alone does not tell us which. If you suspect a worn supply line, ask about repiping.

4

Leaks you cannot see.

A slow leak behind a wall or under a slab can run for weeks. Leak detection is made for that.

5

Water heaters in tight spaces.

The U.S. Department of Energy lists tank units at roughly 10 to 15 years. Our water heater replacement page explains how we size a new one.

Checks worth doing in an older house.

Look under sinks and around the water heater for stains, rust or damp cabinet floors, since small leaks show up there first. Run each faucet and note any that sputter or run weak, because that can point to a worn valve or a clogged aerator.

Pay attention to drains that gurgle or smell, and to a toilet that keeps refilling. These are cheap signs to catch early. If you see one, a drain cleaning visit or a quick fixture repair is usually less costly than waiting for a backup.

When you are not sure what you are looking at, call us and describe it. We would rather tell you it can wait than have you guess.

Sewer, water and what we could not confirm.

Town zoning for both Village districts requires development to attach to public water and sanitary sewer. That is a rule for development, not proof of which utility serves your address, and we could not find a source that names the Village water provider.

Please check your bill for the provider. If you are not sure whether a problem is in your own line or in the main, call us before you dig, and we will help you work out who is responsible.

Before you call.

In an older house the main shutoff is often in a basement or crawl space. Turn it clockwise until it stops, and keep drains idle if one is backing up. If you smell gas, go outside and call the gas utility or 911.

To check for a hidden leak, the state utility consumer office suggests watching your water meter while every fixture is off. The EPA estimates the average household loses about 10,000 gallons a year to leaks.

When a toilet problem is an emergency.

A bowl that is rising toward the rim, water pouring from the base or tank, or a cracked bowl that keeps leaking cannot wait until Monday. Water on a bathroom floor travels under vinyl and tile and into the ceiling below, so an after-hours leak is worth a night or weekend call.

If other drains gurgle or a tub backs up when you flush, the clog may be in the main line, not the toilet. Stop using every fixture and read our drain cleaning page, or call and describe what you see.

What to do while you wait.

These steps take about five minutes and keep a bad toilet from becoming a bad floor.

1

Stop the water.

Turn the oval handle on the supply line behind the toilet clockwise until it stops. If it spins loosely or will not close, shut off the main water valve.

2

Do not flush again.

Each flush on a blocked line adds about 1.6 gallons or more to the bowl. Lift the tank lid and hold the flapper closed if the bowl is near the top.

3

Protect the floor.

Lay towels around the base and set a bucket under any drip. Move rugs and anything electric away from the wet area.

4

Photograph the damage.

Take photos of the water and any damaged flooring before you clean up. Your insurance agent may ask for them.

5

Note what happened.

Tell us whether it leaks only when flushed, all the time, or only at the base. That one detail points us to the right part.

Why a quiet toilet leak costs more than it looks.

The EPA's WaterSense program says the average household's leaks waste more than 9,300 gallons a year, and toilets are often the culprit, usually a worn flapper. The Indiana Office of Utility Consumer Counselor offers a simple test. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank, wait 15 to 20 minutes without flushing, and color in the bowl means a leak.

You can also read your water meter before and after two hours with nothing running. If the numbers change, something is leaking. Your water bill names your utility, and an unusually high bill with no explanation is a good reason to test every toilet in the house.

Signs your toilet needs repair.

Most toilet problems announce themselves. The sound, the timing and the location of the water narrow the cause.

Runs after you flush.

A hiss or trickle that never fully stops usually means a worn flapper or a fill valve that does not shut off.

Refills on its own.

The tank tops up every few minutes with nobody nearby. Water is slipping past the flapper into the bowl.

Water at the base.

A wet ring around the bottom of the toilet points to a failed wax ring, a loose bowl or a cracked flange.

Rocks or wobbles.

A toilet that shifts when you sit has loose closet bolts or a flange that sits low or is damaged.

Clogs again and again.

If plunging only helps for a few weeks, the trap, the vent or the line beyond it may be the real problem.

Weak or slow flush.

Mineral buildup in the rim jets or a partly blocked siphon hole starves the bowl of flush force.

Tank sweats or drips.

Moisture on the tank outside is condensation. Water dripping from the tank bolts or the supply connection is a leak.

How we find the cause.

We start with a dye test in the tank to see whether the flapper or flush valve seat is leaking. Then we check the fill valve height and the water level against the overflow tube, because a level set too high runs water down the tube all day.

For a base leak, we look at the wax ring, the closet bolts and the flange. Confirming a bad seal sometimes means lifting the toilet. For repeat clogs we use a closet auger first, and if that does not explain it, a drain camera shows what is in the line.

Repairing or installing a toilet.

Repair and replacement are different jobs. A new flapper, fill valve or flush valve restores most toilets for much less than a new unit, and parts are standard. We replace a toilet instead when the bowl or tank is cracked, the porcelain is chipped near a seal, or the parts are no longer available.

Installing a toilet is more than setting it down. We shut off and drain the old one, pull it, and inspect the flange for cracks, rust and height. Then we set a new wax ring or seal, lower the bowl straight, snug the closet bolts evenly so the porcelain does not crack, and connect a new supply line.

Before you buy, we measure the rough-in, commonly 12 inches from the wall to the center of the flange, so the new toilet fits. We test every flush and check the base for seepage before we leave.

Choosing a new toilet, with the EPA WaterSense facts.

The EPA says toilets account for nearly 30 percent of indoor water use. Old toilets can use as much as 6 gallons per flush. WaterSense labeled models use 1.28 gallons per flush or less, which is 20 percent less than the 1.6 gallon federal standard.

The EPA estimates that replacing old toilets can cut toilet water use 20 to 60 percent, nearly 13,000 gallons a year for an average family, and save more than $170 a year in water costs. Your own savings depend on how old the current toilet is and how many people use it. We do not promise a figure for your bill.

Running and leaking toilets.

A running toilet is almost always one of three parts. The flapper no longer seals, the chain is too tight or too loose, or the fill valve does not shut off at the right level. EPA WaterSense says easily corrected leaks can save about 10 percent on a water bill. A flapper kit is a small repair, but you need to match the flush valve size, so bring the old part or call us.

What affects the price of toilet repair.

A toilet job can be a part swap or a floor repair, and we price it after we know which.

Part or whole toilet.

A flapper or fill valve is a small repair. A new toilet adds the fixture, the removal of the old one and the seal.

Flange condition.

A cracked or low flange needs a repair ring or a new flange before the toilet can seal.

Floor damage.

A leak that has softened subfloor or lifted tile adds work beyond the toilet.

Clog depth.

A clog at the trap is quick. One that sits farther down the line needs a longer cable or a camera.

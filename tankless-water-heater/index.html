How a tankless water heater works.

When you open a hot tap, a flow sensor tells the unit that water is moving. The burner or elements fire, and the heat exchanger raises the temperature as the water passes through. Close the tap and the unit shuts down, so nothing is kept hot between uses.

Every unit has a maximum temperature rise at a given flow rate. Ask for more hot water than it can heat, say two showers and a dishwasher at once, and the temperature drops. That limit explains a lot of complaints that are not faults at all.

Signs your tankless unit needs attention.

Start with what you notice at the tap. The pattern usually tells us where to look first.

Slow to heat.

Hot water takes a long time to reach a far bathroom. Some of that wait is cold water already sitting in the pipe, but a scaled exchanger or a clogged inlet screen can add to it.

Temperature swings.

Hot, then lukewarm, then hot again, often when a second fixture opens. Flow limits, a weak flow sensor, scale or a gas supply that is too small can all cause this.

The cold water sandwich.

A burst of hot water, a short cold stretch, then hot again after you pause a shower. It is a known tankless habit and sometimes improved with settings or a recirculation setup.

An error code on the display.

Codes usually point to ignition, flame sensing, venting or flow. Write the code down, because the manual and the unit's error history narrow the cause quickly.

Weak hot flow only.

Cold water runs normally but hot is thin. A partly blocked inlet filter screen or heat exchanger is the usual suspect.

Rumbling or popping.

Scale can make water boil in small pockets inside the exchanger. That sound is a descaling conversation, not a reason to ignore the unit.

Damp spots at the unit.

Leaks at a union, a service valve or the condensate line leave moisture below the heater. Shut the unit off and take a photo before you call.

If you smell gas or see water pouring from the unit.

A gas smell means leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not flip switches or look for the source. We come after the gas company says the home is safe.

For a steady leak, turn off the gas or power to the unit, then close the cold-water isolation valve below it. If that valve is missing or will not turn, close the main water shutoff, usually where the line enters the house.

Why winter changes tankless performance in Zionsville.

NWS climate normals for Indianapolis, the nearest long-term station, give a January average low of 20.9 F. Water entering a house in winter is usually colder than in summer, so the unit must add more heat to reach the same setting. A shower that stays hot in July can run lukewarm in January at the same flow.

If that matches your house, ask us to compare the unit's maximum temperature rise with the flow of your fixtures. Also check where the unit sits. Most tankless heaters rely on electrical freeze protection, so one in an unheated garage or crawl space can freeze during a power outage in a hard cold snap. Your manual lists the rules for your model.

How we find why a tankless unit underperforms.

We start with the display history and the code table in the manual. Then we check the basics in order. That means the inlet filter screen for debris, hot and cold flow at a fixture, gas pressure on a manometer while the unit fires, and the vent and combustion air path.

A flow sensor or temperature sensor that reads wrong gets tested before it is replaced. If the heat exchanger looks scaled, we confirm with flow and temperature rise readings, then talk through descaling. You hear what we found before any parts are ordered.

Flushing and descaling a tankless water heater.

Minerals come out of the water when it is heated, and the narrow passages of a tankless heat exchanger collect them as scale. The early signs are longer waits for hot water, lower flow, noise and error codes. Flushing pushes a manufacturer-approved descaling solution through the exchanger so those deposits dissolve and rinse away.

A proper flush needs isolation valves with drain ports on the hot and cold lines, plus a small pump and hoses. Many units were installed without them, and we can add the valves during the same visit. We also clean the inlet screen, because debris there looks like scale at the tap.

How often to flush depends on your water and your maker's manual. Your water bill names your utility, and its annual water quality report lists hardness. You can also test your tap with a kit from a hardware store. If the water is hard, softening or filtration ahead of the heater can slow scale, though that is a separate decision.

Descaling has limits. It cannot repair a cracked exchanger, a failed circuit board or a leaking fitting, and a unit that has gone years without a flush may not recover fully. We tell you which case you are in.

Tankless or tank, an honest comparison.

Tankless is a good fit for some houses and a poor one for others. We cannot promise savings on your bills, because they depend on how much hot water you use and what your fuel costs.

What tankless does well.

  • DOE lists about 20 years of life, against 10 to 15 for tank models.
  • No stored tank to keep hot between uses.
  • Wall-mounted, which frees floor space.
  • Hot water does not run out during one long shower.

What to weigh before switching.

  • Gas models often need a larger gas line and special venting.
  • Installation work is bigger than a like-for-like tank swap.
  • Output is limited when many fixtures run or the incoming water is cold.
  • Units need periodic flushing, and savings are not guaranteed.

What a tankless installation involves.

A gas tankless unit draws far more gas at full fire than a tank heater, so the existing gas line is often too small. We size it from the unit's gas input and the length of the run, which can mean new pipe from the meter. Venting is the other big item, since many units need sealed vent runs to the outside, and condensing models drain acidic condensate that needs a proper disposal path.

Electric tankless units pull heavy current and may need a dedicated circuit, which is licensed electrician work. Permit rules vary, so ask the Zionsville Planning & Building Department before work starts. If you are swapping a tank for a tankless, our water heater installation page covers the planned-install side, and plumbing installation covers new gas and water lines.

What affects the price of tankless water heater work.

Tankless jobs vary more than almost any plumbing work, so we quote after we see the house.

Repair or installation.

A descale or sensor swap is a service visit. A new unit adds the heater, the labor to set it and the removal of the old one.

Gas line size.

If the run from the meter cannot feed a tankless unit at full fire, the line has to be upsized.

Venting and condensate.

Vent material, run length and where the condensate drains all change the scope.

Isolation valves.

Adding service valves lets the unit be flushed, and it adds a little to the first visit.

Access and location.

A unit in an open utility room is simpler than one in a tight closet or an unheated garage.

Standard homeowner policies usually cover sudden and accidental water damage, such as a burst pipe, and usually do not cover gradual leaks, wear or the failed part itself. Check your policy and ask your agent.

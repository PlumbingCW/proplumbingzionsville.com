When a commercial plumbing problem is an emergency.

A business has a different threshold for urgent than a home does. A single clogged restroom sink can wait for a morning appointment. A floor drain pushing water up in a commercial kitchen cannot, because a kitchen with a backed-up floor drain often has to stop cooking until it is fixed.

Call us right away for an active leak you cannot stop, any sewage backing up from a floor drain or restroom, no water at all to the building, or water near electrical panels or equipment. We take these calls at night, on weekends and on holidays, and we book same-day visits for less urgent jobs when the schedule allows.

What to do while you wait.

Do what is safe from the list below and leave the rest. Staff safety comes before stock.

1

Find and close the main shutoff.

In a multi-tenant building the main may serve every suite, so tell the property manager before you close it. A tenant-only shutoff, if one exists, is the better first move.

2

Kill power near the water.

If water is reaching outlets, a panel or a kitchen appliance, shut off that breaker only if you can reach it dry. Otherwise stay back and say so when you call.

3

Stop using the fixtures.

Do not flush, run dishwashers or pour more down a backing-up drain. Every gallon you add has to go somewhere, and it is usually the floor.

4

Protect stock and records.

Lift boxes onto pallets or shelving, unplug equipment on the floor and move paper files. Water wicks up cardboard and drywall faster than most people expect.

5

Document before you clean.

Take photos and a short video of the source, the water line on the walls and the damaged items. Your insurer and your landlord will both ask for them.

Signs a commercial building has a hidden leak.

Large buildings hide leaks well. A suite that nobody enters over a weekend can lose water for two days before anyone sees it.

Meter moves after hours.

Check the water meter when the building is empty and every fixture is off. If the dial or digital reading keeps changing, water is escaping somewhere.

Stained ceiling tiles.

A brown ring or a sagging tile points to water above, but the source is often several feet away along a pipe or joist.

Musty smell in one area.

Damp drywall and carpet pad smell before they look wet. A smell that stays after cleaning is worth a closer look.

Warm or soft floor spots.

A warm patch can mean a hot water line leaking under a slab. A soft or lifting floor suggests water has been sitting a while.

Pressure drops in one suite.

Low pressure at a single tenant space, while others are normal, can mean a leak or a partly closed valve on that branch.

A water bill that jumps.

A sudden rise with no change in how the business operates is often the first clue. Compare the last few bills before you call.

Commercial leak detection without tearing up the building.

We start with the simplest tests. Shut every fixture, read the meter, wait, and read it again. If it moves, we isolate zones by closing valves one branch at a time until the reading stops, which narrows a leak to a wing, a floor or a single run of pipe.

Then we pinpoint it with listening equipment, a thermal camera for hot water lines and a pressure test on the isolated branch. Where a drain line is suspected, a drain camera shows cracks and offsets from the inside. The goal is to open one small section of wall or floor instead of several.

We will tell you when a leak cannot be found without more invasive work. Sometimes the better answer is to re-run a short section of pipe rather than chase a pinhole inside a slab. Our leak detection service covers the same methods for homes.

Floor drains, grease and commercial drain service.

Restaurants and cafes send fats, oils and grease down the line every hour. Grease cools, sticks to pipe walls and hardens, so the drain narrows a little each week until a Friday rush finds the limit. A kitchen with a grease trap or interceptor needs it emptied and cleaned on a schedule that matches its volume.

How often depends on seating, menu and local requirements, so ask your landlord's lease terms and the Town of Zionsville which rules apply to your space. We can clean and inspect a trap, check that its baffles and lid seal are intact, and clear the line beyond it. For stubborn buildup, hydro jetting scours the pipe wall instead of poking a hole through the blockage.

Floor drains in shop areas and mechanical rooms matter too. A floor drain that has dried out lets sewer gas up, and one that backs up usually points to a main line problem. We can also test and replace a sewage lift pump or ejector that moves restroom waste from a below-grade level up to the sewer line.

Backflow assemblies and testing for businesses.

A backflow preventer keeps water that has been in your building, including irrigation water, boiler water or a carbonator line, from being pulled back into the public supply. Many commercial properties have one on the main, on a fire line or on an irrigation system.

Testing is planned work, not an emergency, so we schedule it like a normal service call. Which assemblies need testing, how often, and who receives the report depends on your water utility and the Town. Ask your utility and the Zionsville Planning & Building Department before you book, and tell us the make and size of the assembly if you know it.

We can test an assembly, repair worn internal parts or replace one that fails. We do not skip a failed test, and we will not sign off on a device that is not working.

Owner, tenant and landlord: who handles the plumbing.

Lease language decides who pays, so read yours before the problem, not during it.

Usually the building owner or landlord.

  • Main supply and the underground line from the meter to the building (the Indiana OUCC says that line is typically the customer's responsibility).
  • Shared drain and sewer lines serving several suites.
  • Building-wide water heaters and boilers.
  • Hidden leaks inside walls and ceilings.

Often the tenant.

  • Fixtures inside the leased space.
  • Clogs caused by the tenant's own use, such as grease or wipes.
  • Tenant-installed equipment like dishwashers and ice machines.
  • Grease trap service in a restaurant space.

What we do not do.

We do not work on industrial process piping, medical gas, fire sprinkler systems or large high-rise mechanical plants. We do not design a building's plumbing from scratch, and we will not pull a permit for work that needs a different licensed trade.

If a call falls outside our scope, we will say so on the phone instead of sending someone who cannot finish it. For a restaurant remodel or tenant build-out, ask the Zionsville Planning & Building Department which permits apply, then tell us what the plans call for.

What affects the price of commercial plumbing work.

Commercial jobs vary more than residential ones, so we quote after we understand the building.

Access and hours.

Working in a ceiling above a dining room or after closing takes planning. Night and weekend work is priced differently from a weekday call, and we say so before we start.

Pipe size and material.

A half-inch copper branch is a different job from a four-inch cast iron drain. Larger pipe means heavier parts and more labor.

Number of fixtures or zones.

Testing one backflow assembly is a single visit. Locating a leak across several suites takes time zone by zone.

Permits and inspections.

Some commercial work needs a permit. The Zionsville Planning & Building Department can tell you what applies to your project.

Parts and equipment.

A lift pump, a grease interceptor or a backflow assembly each have their own cost, and the right size depends on your load.

A commercial property policy usually responds to sudden, accidental water damage like a burst supply line. It usually does not pay for slow leaks, wear or the failed part itself. Check your policy, ask your agent, and if you rent, ask your landlord what the building's policy covers.

Start with the room, not the model.

The place where the heater sits settles more than the brand does. A gas unit needs a flue path to the outside and enough air for combustion. An electric unit needs a dedicated 240-volt circuit with the right breaker and wire gauge. A heat pump model needs open space around it, and a tankless unit needs a gas line sized for its burner.

A water heater in an attic or on a second floor also needs a drain pan piped to a safe spot, since a leak there lands on a ceiling. We walk the path from the shutoff valve to the vent before choosing anything.

Sizing the tank to the household.

A 40-gallon tank is common in smaller homes, and a 50-gallon is the usual step up for families. The first-hour rating on the yellow EnergyGuide label tells you how much hot water the unit delivers in a busy hour, which is a better guide than the tank volume.

Count your peak hour, not your daily total. If three showers, a dishwasher and a washing machine run back to back in the morning, that hour sets the size. A tank sized too small runs out, and one sized too large keeps more water hot than you use.

Venting, gas lines and electrical, the details that decide the job.

These are the points we check and set before a unit is fastened down.

  • Atmospheric vent A traditional gas heater rises on a draft hood and a metal flue. The flue must slope upward and stay clear of combustibles.
    .
  • Power vent or direct vent A fan pushes exhaust through plastic pipe out a side wall. It needs an outlet nearby, and the pipe run is limited by the manufacturer.
    .
  • Gas supply A shutoff valve, a drip leg and a flexible connector sit on the line, and every joint gets a soap-solution leak test.
    .
  • Electrical An electric tank needs its own breaker and a disconnect within reach, wired to the manufacturer's listed size.
    .
  • Combustion air A heater in a tight closet can starve for air. Backdrafting pulls exhaust into the room, which is why vent draft is checked after startup.
    .

Expansion tank, relief valve and drain line.

Water expands when it is heated. On a closed system (one with a check valve or pressure reducing valve at the meter), that extra volume has nowhere to go, and pressure climbs on every heating cycle. A small thermal expansion tank on the cold line takes up the swing. Its air charge is set to match your house pressure before it goes in.

The temperature and pressure relief valve is the last safety. It opens at 150 psi or 210 degrees F, and its discharge pipe must run downward to a floor drain or outside, with no valve, cap or threaded end. Never plug it. A drain valve at the base lets the tank be flushed later.

Planning around hard water and winter.

Mineral-heavy water builds scale on elements and burner surfaces, and it shortens the life of a tank or clogs a tankless heat exchanger. Test your tap with a kit, and ask your water utility for its annual water quality report. If your water is hard, a softener upstream of the heater may be worth the cost, and our water softener page covers that.

In Indianapolis the January average low is 20.9 F. Cold incoming water makes the unit work harder. A heater in an attic, an unheated garage or against an exterior wall needs insulated lines, because those spaces put pipes at risk of freezing.

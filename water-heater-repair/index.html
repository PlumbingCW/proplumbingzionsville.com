When a leaking water heater is an emergency.

A tank holds 40 or 50 gallons in most homes, and a failed tank can release all of it onto the floor within an hour or so. Water that is steaming, spraying from the top, or spreading toward a finished basement, a furnace or an electrical panel needs action right now. A slow drip from a pipe fitting is less urgent, but it still gets worse.

A gas smell is a different matter. Leave the house, then call the gas utility or 911 from outside. Do not flip switches or relight anything on the way out.

What to do while you wait.

These steps take about five minutes and limit the damage before a plumber reaches you.

1

Cut the energy.

For an electric tank, turn off its breaker. For gas, turn the control dial to OFF or close the gas valve on the supply pipe. Never leave an element powered in an empty tank, because it burns out in minutes.

2

Close the cold supply.

Turn the valve on the cold pipe above the tank clockwise until it stops. If it will not move, close the main shutoff where the line enters the house.

3

Contain the water.

Move boxes off the floor, lay towels, and use a wet vacuum if you own one. Open a hot tap upstairs so the lines drain and the pressure drops.

4

Document before cleanup.

Take photos of the tank, the data label and the wet area. Your insurance agent will ask for them, and a ruined tank is easier to price when the model number is visible.

5

Leave the drain valve alone.

Draining a full tank onto a finished floor makes the mess bigger. Wait for us unless you can run a hose to a floor drain.

Signs your water heater needs repair.

Each symptom below points toward a different part, which is why guessing at a replacement is expensive.

Water at the base.

Check whether it comes from the tank body, the top fittings or the relief valve pipe. Only a leaking tank wall is a replacement. Everything else is usually a repair.

Hot water runs out fast.

On an electric unit this often means a dead lower element. On gas it can mean a thick layer of sediment on the tank floor.

Lukewarm water only.

A bad thermostat, a tripped high-limit switch or a weak gas burner can each hold the temperature below where it should sit.

Rumbling or popping.

Water trapped under hardened sediment boils and bursts through it. The noise is a flush job, not a sign the tank is about to fail.

Rusty or metallic hot water.

If only the hot side runs rusty, the tank lining or anode rod is wearing out. Cold water that runs rusty too points at the pipes.

Pilot will not stay lit.

A worn thermocouple, a dirty pilot tube or a draft problem are the usual causes on older gas models.

Rotten egg smell.

Bacteria feeding on a worn anode rod can make hot water smell of sulfur. It is a nuisance, not a hazard, and a new rod often cures it.

Where the leak is coming from decides the repair.

Three places account for most wet floors. The first is the connections on top, where flex lines or copper stubs meet the tank. A loose union or a worn gasket is a short job. The second is the temperature and pressure relief valve, which is built to open at 150 psi or 210 degrees F. A valve that drips constantly may be tired, or reacting to excess pressure.

The third place is the tank itself. Steel tanks rust from the inside, and once the lining fails, water seeps through the seam and runs down the jacket. That leak cannot be patched, and we will say so rather than sell a repair that will not last. If it is the tank, our water heater replacement page explains what comes next.

How we find the failed part.

We start by drying the tank and wrapping a dry paper towel around each fitting, which shows a seep better than eyes alone. A digital multimeter checks both heating elements and the thermostats on an electric unit, since a good element reads a specific resistance and a burnt one reads open. On gas, we measure thermocouple output and look at the burner flame and flue draft.

A pressure gauge on a hose bib tells us the house water pressure. Municipal supply above about 80 psi stresses every fixture in the house, and it can open a relief valve that is otherwise healthy. We also check whether a closed-loop system lacks an expansion tank, because heated water has nowhere to go and the valve takes the blame.

No hot water, and what usually causes it.

Start with the simple checks. On electric models, look at the breaker and press the red reset button on the upper thermostat behind the access panel (power off first). On gas, check that the pilot is lit and the gas valve is open. If neither explains it, the fault is inside the unit.

Electric tanks carry two elements, usually 4,500 watts at 240 volts, and one failing gives you hot water for a short time only. A gas unit that lights and then drops out often has a bad thermocouple. A modern unit with an igniter may show a blinking light code, and the pattern in the owner's manual narrows the cause before we open anything.

Repairing electric, gas and hybrid heaters.

The fault list changes with the energy source, so the repair does too.

Electric and hybrid (heat pump).

  • Conventional electric: failed elements, thermostats and high-limit switches are the common parts.
  • Hybrid models add a compressor, a fan and an air filter that clogs and trips an error.
  • A hybrid needs open air around it, and a closed closet starves the heat pump of warmth.
  • Condensate drain lines on hybrids can clog and overflow onto the floor.

Gas.

  • Pilot assemblies, thermocouples and igniters wear out first.
  • A gas control valve is a costlier part, so a repair has to be weighed against the age of the tank.
  • Flue and draft trouble can cause backdrafting, so we check venting on every gas call.
  • Burner ports can foul with dust and sediment, and a yellow flame points at that.

Noise, sediment and routine upkeep.

Sediment settles at the bottom of every tank, and the more minerals in your supply, the faster it builds. Ask your water utility for its annual water quality report, or test your tap with a store-bought kit. Citizens' Indianapolis-area water is hard, and the Town's utilities page lists Citizens Water contact numbers, so your own bill is the place to confirm who serves your address.

A yearly flush through the drain valve clears loose scale, and it keeps the burner or lower element working in clean water instead of buried in crust. The anode rod is a magnesium or aluminum rod that rusts so the tank does not, so check it every few years. A flush on a tank never serviced in ten years can start leaks at the drain valve, and we will say when to leave one alone.

Service, flushing and error codes.

Many calls are maintenance, not breakdowns. A service visit means flushing the tank, testing the relief valve, checking the anode rod and looking over venting and gas connections. A blinking error code on a newer unit is the heater reporting which sensor or ignition step failed, and the manual lists what each pattern means.

What affects the price of water heater repair.

Repair prices run from a small valve job to a gas control valve, so a number over the phone without seeing the unit would be a guess.

The failed part.

A relief valve, an element and a gas control valve differ in cost, and some models need a part ordered.

Fuel type and model.

Hybrid and power-vent models carry costlier components than a basic electric tank.

Access.

A unit in a tight utility closet or a finished basement takes longer to reach and work around.

Age and condition.

On an older tank, we may recommend against a part that will not outlast the next failure.

After-hours timing.

Night, weekend and holiday calls can price differently from a scheduled visit, and we tell you before work starts.

Repiping in Zionsville, IN.

Repiping in Zionsville, IN means replacing the water supply pipes inside a house, either the whole system or one failing section, with new copper, PEX or CPVC. It is done when patching stops working, usually because the pipe is corroded from the inside, leaking in several places or restricting flow.

This is planned work, not a same-hour repair. If a pipe is actively leaking, shut off the main first and call our 24/7 emergency line, then we can talk about whether the whole system needs replacing.

Signs your pipes are failing.

One leak is a repair. A pattern of leaks, or a change in the water itself, is the kind of evidence that points toward replacement.

Repeated pinhole leaks.

A second or third leak within a year or two, in different spots on the same material, usually means the pipe is wearing out everywhere, not at one fitting.

Brown or rust-tinted water.

Water that runs orange or brown from the tap after sitting overnight can come from corroding steel pipe. It clears as you run it, then comes back.

Weak flow at every fixture.

Galvanized steel builds scale inside until a three-quarter-inch pipe passes water like a much smaller one. Showers go weak first, especially when a second tap opens.

Green or blue crusting.

Greenish buildup on copper joints and valves is corrosion. A small patch is cosmetic, but wet crust around a joint is a slow leak.

Pipes that rattle or clank.

Loose straps and water hammer are fixable. Noise together with leaks and discolored water is a different conversation.

A bill that creeps up.

Hidden leaks inside walls waste water long before stains show. Check the meter with everything off, as the EPA suggests, to see if it moves.

Why pipes in a house wear out.

Galvanized steel is zinc-coated iron. The coating wears away over decades and the iron underneath rusts from the inside, so the pipe narrows even when the outside looks fine. Copper can develop pinhole leaks where water chemistry, flow speed or poor soldering has thinned the wall. Plastic supply pipes have their own limits, including age and fittings that were never well suited to the pipe.

Water quality plays a part, and it is not something we can give you from a table. Ask your water utility for its annual water quality report, and check your bill to see which utility that is. If you have pinhole leaks in copper, tell us when you call so we can look at the pattern.

Partial repipe or whole house.

You do not always need to replace everything. The choice turns on how much of the system is the same worn material.

Partial repipe makes sense when.

  • One bathroom, kitchen or a single branch has failed.
  • The rest of the pipe tests sound and flows well.
  • A section was damaged by a freeze or a remodel.
  • You plan to renovate the other areas later.

Whole-house repipe makes sense when.

  • Leaks keep appearing in different places.
  • The same galvanized or failing pipe runs through the whole house.
  • Flow is poor at every fixture.
  • You are opening walls anyway for a major remodel.

How we decide what needs replacing.

We start by looking, not selling. We trace the supply from the main shutoff through the basement or crawl space, note every material and fitting we can see, and check pressure and flow at several fixtures. Where we find leaks, we look at the pipe around them, not just the drip.

A pressure test on isolated branches shows whether a section holds. Where access is limited, a small camera or a thermal scan can show a hot water line leaking inside a wall. The result is a written plan for what to replace, in what order, and what can stay.

Drywall, access and what repiping really involves.

Repiping usually means opening some drywall. How much depends on the house. Homes with open basements or crawl spaces let us run most new pipe below the floor and reach each fixture from underneath, so wall openings stay small and local.

In a finished two-story house, new vertical runs may need access holes at floor and ceiling lines. We cut them clean and neat so a drywall finisher can patch them. We do not promise zero holes, and we do not do the painting, so plan for a drywall and paint trade after us.

The water is off for part of each day while we work. We tell you in advance which days the main will be closed, so you can plan around showers, laundry and cooking.

Galvanized pipe replacement in older homes.

Galvanized steel is the clearest case for replacement. Its failure mode is internal, so the pipe can look fine on the outside and still pass only a trickle. Steel nipples and threaded joints also tend to seize and crack when disturbed, which is why plumbers avoid tapping into old galvanized with a quick fix.

When we replace it, we also replace the valves and connections that join it, because mixing new copper directly onto steel invites corrosion at the joint without the right fittings. If you have a mix of old materials, we map the full system first so the new work ties in cleanly.

What affects the price of repiping.

Repiping is priced per house, not per foot, because no two houses are alike. We do not quote over the phone without seeing the pipe.

Pipe material.

Copper, PEX and CPVC differ in material cost and in how long they take to install. We show you the options for your layout.

Size and layout of the house.

More bathrooms, floors and fixtures mean more pipe and more connections. A compact ranch is a different job from a three-story house.

Access.

An open basement saves labor. Finished ceilings and tight walls take longer, and that shows up in the price.

Permits and inspection.

Some work needs a permit and inspection. The Planning & Building Department can say what applies to your project.

Repairs after the work.

Drywall patching and painting are separate trades. We tell you what is included in our price and what is not.

Home policies generally respond to a pipe that bursts suddenly. They generally do not pay to replace pipe that has simply worn out or leaked slowly. Read your policy and ask your agent before you assume a repipe is covered.

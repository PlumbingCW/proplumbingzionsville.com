When a broken water line is an emergency.

Call right away when water is bubbling up in the yard, a sinkhole or soft patch is opening near the line, or a basement is taking water where the pipe enters the foundation. A full break can move a lot of water in an hour, and it can wash soil out from under a driveway or slab.

A sudden loss of pressure at every tap, especially with a meter that is spinning, points the same way. The line is staffed after hours, on weekends and on holidays, and a plumber can talk you through the shutoff before anyone arrives.

What to do while you wait.

These steps limit the damage and make the repair faster.

1

Close the main shutoff.

Look where the pipe enters the foundation, commonly in a basement or crawl space. Turn the valve clockwise until it stops.

2

Check the meter.

With every fixture off, a moving meter dial or a spinning flow indicator means water is still escaping on your side of the line.

3

Look for the curb stop or meter pit.

A shutoff in the yard may belong to the utility. Do not force it, and call the water utility on your bill about anything on the meter side.

4

Keep people and vehicles off soft ground.

A void under a driveway or lawn can give way. Mark the spot and stay clear.

5

Photograph the damage.

Take pictures of standing water, the meter reading and any affected rooms before cleanup, for your insurer.

Why water service lines fail here.

Indiana's Office of Utility Consumer Counselor says the buried supply pipe from the meter to the building typically falls to the customer. A leak on this stretch is yours to fix, and yours to pay for in water used. Before spending money, call the water utility on your bill to confirm where your side starts.

Winter does most of the damage in this region. Indianapolis averages a January low of 20.9 F, and the city recorded -27 F in January 1994. Repeated freezing and thawing moves the soil around a pipe, and old fittings, corroded steel and aged joints give out where that movement concentrates.

Signs the water service line is leaking.

The line is out of sight, so the clues show up elsewhere.

The meter moves with everything off.

Shut off every fixture and appliance. If the dial still turns, water is escaping somewhere past the meter.

An unexplained rise in the bill.

A sustained jump in water use without a change in habits can point to a leak in the supply line.

A wet or soft spot in the yard.

Grass that stays green and soggy in dry weather, or a muddy patch between the meter and house, marks a likely path.

Low pressure at every tap.

When both hot and cold weaken together, the problem is upstream of the water heater.

Hissing near the entry point.

A faint hiss near the wall where the pipe comes in suggests a leak under pressure close to the house.

Muddy or discolored water.

A break can pull soil into the pipe, and corroded steel can shed rust.

How we find a buried line leak.

We start with the meter. We shut off the house, read the dial, and wait. Then we put a pressure gauge on an outdoor hose bib to see whether the line holds pressure when the valve is closed.

If the line is leaking, acoustic listening gear on the pipe, valve and meter tells us roughly where the sound is loudest. A pipe locator traces the route, so we dig in one place, not four. Our page on leak detection covers the same tools for leaks inside walls and slabs.

Service line leaks and buried pipe.

A buried leak does not always surface. Water can travel along the pipe trench or drain away into gravel, so a dry yard does not mean a dry line. A leak the size of a pencil lead still adds up, since the EPA reports that the average household leaks waste more than 9,300 gallons a year across all fixtures.

Materials matter too. Copper, galvanized steel, polyethylene and older lead lines all behave differently. Steel rusts shut from the inside, copper pits or splits, and plastic can crack where it was bent. We identify the pipe at the entry point, then plan the repair around what is really down there. If you suspect lead, ask your water utility about its records and testing.

Low water pressure from the service line.

Low pressure has several causes, and the supply line is only one. A pinched or corroded service pipe restricts flow at every tap. A partly closed main valve, a failing pressure regulator or a clogged aerator can do the same on a smaller scale.

We check static pressure at the hose bib, then pressure with a tap running, because a big drop when water flows suggests a restriction. If only one fixture is weak, the problem is local to that fixture, which our faucet repair work covers. If the utility's side is weak, we tell you to call them and explain what you saw.

The meter, the main and who to call.

The meter belongs to the water utility. For anything about the meter, the meter pit or the main in the street, call the water utility named on your bill. We repair the line from the meter side into your house, not the utility's equipment.

If you have a private well, there is no utility meter. Boone County Health Department requires a permit to drill or repair a private well and registered well drillers, so contact them first. For a municipal connection, a large water main replacement is a utility project and not something we do on a home service call.

Digging rules and permits.

Indiana 811 receives your notice and passes it on to utility operators. The notice must arrive at least two full working days before digging begins and no more than ten calendar days before. A working day runs 7 a.m. to 6 p.m. Indianapolis time, excluding weekends and legal holidays.

A ticket expires 20 days after it is submitted, so an old ticket may need renewing. Because a leak repair is often urgent, the work is planned around these timing rules. Permit needs depend on the job, so ask the Zionsville Planning & Building Department.

What affects the price of water line repair.

Costs vary with the line, the ground and the method. These are the main drivers.

Length of line.

The distance from meter to house sets how much pipe and digging a replacement needs.

Depth and ground.

Deeper lines and rocky or wet soil take longer to expose.

Surface above the pipe.

Lawn is the simplest. A driveway, sidewalk or patio adds cutting and restoration.

Pipe material.

Copper and polyethylene differ in material cost, fittings and how well they suit trenchless methods.

Emergency timing.

Night and weekend calls involve different staffing than a scheduled daytime visit.

Sudden damage, such as a break that floods a room, may be covered. Seepage over months, or the pipe itself, generally is not. Some insurers sell a service line endorsement, so ask your agent what yours includes and read the policy.
